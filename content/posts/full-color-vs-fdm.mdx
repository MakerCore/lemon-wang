On October 22, in Shanghai, a customer asked me a question.

He runs a children's math board game — an Australian company that manufactures in China, with a few of their own people on the ground there. One of their games had just finished a crowdfunding run at 2000% of its goal — across 54 countries, by his account. Halfway through the meeting he stopped and asked:

> "After that crowdfunding blew up, I suddenly felt — will we just not need this thing anymore?"
> *— the customer, 2026-10-22, ~26:07, translated from Mandarin*

"This thing" was the FDM printer — the cheap plastic kind his players use to print the game pieces at home. He was asking whether full-color printing would replace it.

I answered on the spot:

> "In the short term it won't replace it. But in some scenarios there will be real competition — in the home, a bit. For industrial parts, or a guy in his garage printing a wrench, he won't use us — that's not what we do. What we actually solve is the color problem."
> *— my answer, 2026-10-22, ~26:25, translated and lightly condensed from Mandarin*

I work on full-color printing. That makes its limits part of my job.

---

## The wrong test

"Will full-color replace FDM?" is the wrong test. Different material systems don't prevent competition — they change where substitution makes sense.

FDM melts a thermoplastic and extrudes it through a nozzle. No light involved. It offers a broad range of thermoplastics, subject to the printer's temperature and process limits. That's why it's cheap, and why it wins on functional, mechanical parts.

Here, full-color printing means UV-cured material jetting: it deposits photopolymer and cures it with light, reproducing color and texture across the surface as it builds. The color is in the part, not a paint job applied afterward. The material set is narrower and the consumables cost more.

Multi-color FDM remains FDM; it adds discrete filament colors through the printer's material-handling system. It still isn't full-color material jetting, and treating the two as "two kinds of color printing" only muddies the question.

None of this tells you who wins. It only tells you the two processes are different. The customer doesn't choose by technology category. He chooses by the job: appearance requirements, material performance, and what an acceptable part costs to deliver.

---

## Where they compete

So where does substitution actually make sense?

The home is the obvious one. A parent who wants a colorful toy, a hobbyist who wants a finished-looking model without learning to paint — either process can serve that person. Today FDM has the price and the material range, and for a lot of those jobs the bar is "make something cute," not "make something that looks real." Full-color wins where the appearance itself is the deliverable.

Appearance prototypes and small-batch colored models sit in the same zone — jobs where what you're paying for is how the thing looks, not how it performs.

I don't have market data on how wide that overlap is. My read is that FDM currently holds most of it on price. But that's a statement about today's prices, not a law about the two technologies.

---

## Where they don't

Outside appearance-driven work, the two mostly don't touch.

A bracket that carries load. A gear. A housing that sits near heat. The buyer there is counting strength and cost per part, not color, and photopolymer is the wrong material for much of that. FDM's material library is the whole point.

The garage wrench is the example I gave him: the man printing a wrench in his garage won't be my customer. He doesn't want color. He wants a part that holds.

That boundary follows the job, not the technology. Full-color solves the color problem. FDM solves cheap parts that work.

---

## What full-color actually removes

Here's the part worth recosting.

For an FDM part whose required finish can't be achieved with filament colors alone, manual painting adds a whole other workflow: prime, base coat, block in the colors, shade, highlight, varnish. Hand work, one object at a time, and no two come out the same.

That conversation left me with a different question: when does coloring become the production bottleneck?

For the right jobs, full-color printing removes or collapses most of that manual color work — the color is already in the part when it comes out of the machine. Support material still has to be washed off, and whether the surface is "done" depends on the material and the finish the job demands. So it isn't magic, and it isn't "no post-processing." It's one less trade in the chain. Its value depends on how much manual work the required finish would otherwise take.

So the pitch isn't "we replace your printer." It's "we reduce the manual coloring work that happens after the print."

---

## The plain answer

Will full-color replace FDM?

Not in the short term, and not as a category. The processes differ, but their applications overlap. The question that matters isn't "which technology wins" — it's which workflow delivers the required appearance and performance at an acceptable cost, for the job in front of you.

A full-color PM who tells you his technology won't take over the world is giving the only answer that survives a real meeting with a customer who's afraid the thing he just crowdfunded to 2000% is already obsolete.

It isn't. But the work that happens after the print — that's where the money, and the next argument, actually live.
