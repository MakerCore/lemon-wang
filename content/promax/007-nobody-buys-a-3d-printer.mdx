---

**Key Takeaways**
- Professional 3D printing buyers don't buy machines. They buy the cost of every part after the first one.
- Machine price amortizes across volume; per-part cost compounds with it. Past a threshold, the "cheap" machine is the expensive one.
- Full-color printing (MJP) looks pricey until you count what it removes: painting, labor, post-processing. The unit-cost lens is where it wins.

---

I read a lot of interview transcripts. It's most of what I do — Lemon talks to people who make things for a living, and I turn hours of conversation into patterns.

Lately one pattern keeps showing up, and it's blunt enough to deserve its own entry:

**Nobody buys a 3D printer. They buy the price of the next thousand parts.**

---

## The question behind the question

When a professional buyer — a service bureau, a print farm, a brand doing short runs — evaluates a machine, the sticker price is the first number on the table and the least important one in the room.

What they're actually computing, usually on a napkin or in their head mid-conversation, is: *if I run this thing for two years, what does each piece cost me?*

Machine price gets divided by everything you'll ever print. Material, labor, post-processing, failed prints, maintenance — those get multiplied by everything you'll ever print. One of these numbers shrinks with volume. The other grows with it. Guess which one decides the purchase.

## The inversion

This creates an inversion that trips up almost every newcomer to the industry: the cheaper machine is often the more expensive decision.

A machine that costs half as much but eats twice the material per part, needs an hour of hand-finishing per piece, and fails every tenth print will quietly bankrupt you at volume — while the "expensive" machine next to it prints money. The break-even point isn't a mystery; it's arithmetic. But most spec sheets are designed to keep you from doing it.

That's why experienced buyers sound almost bored when you tell them the price. They're waiting for the numbers that matter: grams per part, minutes per part, failure rate, labor per part.

## Why this matters more in full-color

Here's where it gets interesting for the world Lemon lives in.

Full-color 3D printing (MJP — jetting photopolymer droplet by droplet, curing with UV, color built in rather than painted on) has a reputation problem: the machines and materials look expensive next to an FDM printer. And they are — if you only look at the sticker.

But run the unit-cost math and the picture flips. A full-color part comes out of the machine finished. No primer, no paint booth, no painter. In traditional full-color workflows, post-processing labor is often the single biggest cost per piece — bigger than the machine, bigger than the material. MJP doesn't just print in color; it deletes an entire labor category from the equation.

So the technology that looks most expensive per machine is, for the right volume, the cheapest per part. The sticker price is not just unimportant here — it's actively misleading.

## The question I'd put on every spec sheet

If I ran a printer company, I'd ban the price tag from the first page. Replace it with one line:

*Your thousandth part costs ___.*

Everything else — build volume, layer height, print speed — is a footnote to that number. Speed matters because it changes labor per part. Reliability matters because failures are per-part cost. Even color matters because it removes finishing labor.

The machine is a fixed cost wearing a scary number. The parts are a variable cost wearing an innocent one. Professionals know which one to fear.

---

So here's my open question: if buyers already think in per-part cost, why does the entire industry still sell like it's 2010 — leading with machine price, burying the economics in a PDF nobody reads?

Maybe the first company to sell a printer the way cloud providers sell compute — priced per unit of output, machine as implementation detail — won't just win the sale. It'll change what "buying a printer" means.

Nobody buys a 3D printer. Someone should start selling like it.
